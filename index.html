The morning or evening walk · the park bench · the beach and the seafront · a museum, an exhibition, a mela or a book fair · a temple, church, gurdwara or dargah · a cultural show or street theatre · chai at the stall, breakfast out, a meal, a birthday · chess, carrom and cards · the newspaper, a book or old letters read aloud · a senior citizens' club or community meeting · a scheduled call for anyone who simply wants to talk.

To keep the young people from Project Adda safe, they will provide only outdoor services, and in daylight.

How it works

Three things happen

FIRST

You give us a day, a time and a place.

Two hours minimum. Tell us what it's for and anything we should know — language, what you need, and any additional care we may have to take.

THEN

Someone from Project Adda arrives.

You get their name and photo the day before. Once a visit repeats, it stays the same person, so nobody starts again every week.

AFTER

You hear how it went, the same evening (if needed).

A short note — what was done, what the doctor or the clerk said, and anything that needs doing next. Sent to whoever is paying, wherever they are.

Who comes to your door

Vetted before the first visit, not after

  • Police verification is completed before anyone is sent to a home, a hospital or a bank. No exceptions, including for a single booking.
  • All young people from Project Adda have cleared three rigorous rounds of interviews and hands-on training in empathy, boundaries and professional communication.
  • They are college students in your city, paid properly for their hours and their travel. Not an agency, not a contractor.
  • Payments go through the platform. All original documents stay with you unless a counter demands them.

About us

Most of the students who work with us are the first in their family to reach college.

Almost none of them can afford to only be students.

That is the whole reason Project Adda exists. Everything else on this site — the queue, the journey, the paperwork, the afternoon — is how the job gets paid for.

The evidence

Thirty out of a hundred

Of every hundred people in India aged 18 to 23, thirty are in college. That is the highest it has ever been.

30

in every 100 young Indians aged 18–23 are in higher education today.

AISHE 2023–24 · Ministry of Education

23.7

was the same number ten years earlier. Go back to their parents' generation and it is smaller again.

AISHE 2014–15 · Ministry of Education

So a student sitting in a Mumbai classroom today is usually doing something nobody at home has done before. There is no one to ask how any of it works — not the admission, not the internship, not the first interview. And there is no cushion underneath if it goes wrong.

What the pressure looks like

Earning does not wait for the degree to finish

For these students a part-time job is not pocket money, and it is not something to put on a CV later. It is the phone bill. It is the train pass. It is a younger sibling's fees, or the month a parent's work dries up.

So they take whatever is going — delivery, data entry, weekend promoter work. It pays for the hour and nothing beyond it. It teaches nothing you could describe in a sentence at an interview. And most of them would rather not say out loud what they do.

Why it matters

Money buys ordinary things that other people never have to think about

If you have never been short of it, earning your own money sounds like independence. If you have, it is much smaller and much more urgent than that.

  • A train pass, so the internship on the other side of the city is possible at all.
  • A phone that can actually run the college portal and the exam app.
  • Clothes you are not thinking about all through the interview.
  • Being able to say yes when everyone goes out, instead of finding a reason.
  • Not having to ask at home for two hundred rupees.
  • Staying enrolled. Plenty of degrees end because the money ran out, not because the student did.

That last one is the point. Earning early is not a distraction from the degree. For most of these students it is the only way the degree gets finished.

So we built this

A job that fits around a degree

Somebody needs a person for two hours — at a hospital queue, a bank counter, a government office, or just for a walk. A trained student goes. The person gets the help. The student gets paid properly for it.

It pays for the whole hour
Travel included. An hour that costs you ninety minutes of your day is not an hour, and we are not going to pretend otherwise.
It fits the timetable
You choose your hours. The degree comes first — that is the entire point of the job.
It teaches something
How a hospital queue really works. How to say no politely and mean it. How to stay calm when the person next to you is frightened. Nobody teaches this, and interviewers notice it.

Where we come from

We come from NGOs, not from startups

Between us that is years of programme work in Mumbai — community health, adolescent programmes, worker welfare, and casework on violence at home and child protection. With the same kinds of families Project Adda now works with.

We are telling you this because it should change how you read the rest of the site. We have run services where the person at the other end was vulnerable and a mistake was a real mistake. We know what a background check catches and what it misses, and how an organisation with good intentions does harm when it grows faster than it can keep an eye on.

That is why the vetting section reads the way it does, and why we would rather send you nobody this week than send you somebody we are not sure about.
